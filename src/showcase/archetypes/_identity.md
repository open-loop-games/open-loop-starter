# Archetype identities

The registry of identity lines across the set. An archetype is a region the
studio wants a story for, held at three addresses side by side: the market's name
for it (a Steam tag address), a composition in research's pattern language (rows
by handle, from `open-loop-research/LIBRARY.md`), and the exemplar games that
validate the shape ships. How many of those addresses research can ground is the
line's **grounding class**, and the class fixes what the line may cite:

| Class                      | Meaning                                                                         | What the line cites                                                                                                  |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **both**                   | the market names the region and sourced corpus exemplars share a composition    | rows by handle; a LIBRARY composition by name where one exists                                                       |
| **corpus only**            | sourced exemplars share a composition; no tag names the region                  | rows by handle; the rowless component named as such; no tag address                                                  |
| **market, corpus pending** | a tag address names the region; its exemplars are not yet sourced in the corpus | the tag address; rows only where a sourced exemplar carries them; the rowless core named as such; exemplars as a set |
| **neither**                | a starter design claim                                                          | the claim, said as one; the tag region it belongs to by definition; no rows                                          |

A row is cited only where an exemplar carries it. A component the vocabulary
cannot name is stated as rowless, the way LIBRARY § Compositions states its own;
it becomes a candidate for `open-loop-research/patterns/candidate-patterns.md`
only when a refusal is read in source, never from this table. Exemplars are a set
that validates the shape ships in the wild, crosses and all — never lift targets.
A line in the corpus-pending class changes when its exemplars are sourced; the
composition then comes from what they share, not from the built archetype.

The derivation — each tag address, its region, the corpus join and the reading
behind each line — is `open-loop-research/reports/archetype-market-reconciliation.md`,
over the tag snapshots under `open-loop-research/catalog/steam/`. Counts live
there, not here.

## Player experience

### Producer

Producer is the archetype every incremental player already understands within thirty seconds. You click a thing. A number goes up. You spend the number on something that increases the number by itself. Before long you stop clicking and watch the number grow, checking in to buy the next producer tier and the next multiplier upgrade. The texture of play is _pacing against a curve_: costs rise faster than production, so each tier feels unreachable until it isn't, and then it's trivial and you're already saving for the next one.

A thirty-minute session is a sequence of threshold moments — _"I can finally afford a farm"_, _"the cursor upgrade doubled my click value"_, _"the first achievement multiplier just kicked in"_. The long-term pull is modifier stacking: six hours in, a player watches their headline multiplier (base × upgrades × achievements × milestones × tier-bonuses) visibly compound into an unfathomable number, and that is the reward. There is no story; the curve is the content.

### Looper

Looper is the archetype where _a run feels like a rehearsal_. You queue up a sequence of actions inside a strictly limited time budget (a mana pool, a health bar, a countdown), watch the budget drain as each action ticks, and when it's gone the run ends — most of what you built resets, and a thin layer of permanent mastery carries to the next run. The compulsion loop is: _"next run I'll do it faster and further,"_ and over six or eight runs you start doing things you couldn't even reach on run one.

A thirty-minute session is a sequence of queue edits. You notice a skill that's now underpowered, or a new zone you can just barely reach, and reorder your plan accordingly. Between runs you are never idle — you're looking at the queue, at the skill readouts, at the retention track, deciding what run N+1 buys you that run N didn't. The long-term pull is _compounded efficiency_: a first-run player takes ten minutes to reach the second zone; an experienced player reaches it in thirty seconds and spends the remaining mana somewhere deeper. The game is the diff between those two runs.

### Defender

Defender is the archetype of _committing architecture before threat arrives_. You lay towers, traps, or turrets on a grid; you spend a small currency pool on opening placements; then the first wave of enemies arrives and walks a path your layout helped define. You watch the outcome resolve without mid-wave commands. Between waves, you buy another tower, upgrade a range, reshape a lane, and commit again. The texture of play is _tension held across a pause_ — the wave is coming, you have thirty seconds to prepare, and once it starts the board tells you whether your architectural decision was right.

A thirty-minute session is a sequence of wave-clear moments separated by build-phase decisions — the first tower planted, the first round where the last enemy dies before reaching the exit, the first upgrade that changes which enemy type you can handle, the first stage cleared. The long-term pull is vertical: later towers punch harder, later waves escalate to force new answers, and the roster of towers you know how to combine becomes your expressive vocabulary. The player role is _architect_, not participant.

### Scaler

Scaler is the archetype where _the game you played an hour ago becomes a footnote_. You buy producers, they produce, costs scale, a number climbs — familiar Producer shape. But then you hit a prestige threshold and ascend: everything you built resets, and in exchange you get a new currency, a new upgrade tree, and a multiplier that makes your next run minutes instead of hours. A few runs later there is a _second_ layer on top of the first, with its own currency and its own reset scope that wipes the first layer's upgrades too. Eventually a third. Each layer compresses the one below it until what took you an hour takes seconds.

A thirty-minute session near the start is a prestige cycle: push the headline resource past a threshold, click the big button, watch the multipliers compound, out-perform your previous run within minutes. A thirty-minute session in the mid-game is a _challenge_ run — you voluntarily take on a nerf ("production halved for Dimension 8," "can't buy upgrades") inside the outer prestige loop, grinding it out for a permanent stacking bonus. The long-term pull is watching the headline multiplier readout scroll past `1e12×`, then `1e120×`, then into tetration, knowing every one of those digits came from a deliberate composition of layers, challenges, and upgrades you designed yourself.

### Interlocker

Interlocker is the archetype where _you don't watch a number, you watch a system_. You start with one raw resource and one activity that produces it. Five minutes in you unlock a building that consumes that resource to produce a second — now you have an input and an output, a _ratio_, and a reason to care about rates. Ten minutes later a third building wants both previous resources at once; now you're balancing. By the time you have four or five buildings feeding each other, you've stopped thinking about any single resource and started thinking about the _graph_ — which node is the bottleneck, which edge is starving, which pair of buildings multiplies each other when placed on the same tech tier.

A thirty-minute session in Interlocker is a sequence of structural moments: _"I finally unlocked Workshop,"_ _"the refinery chain is making plastic,"_ _"I don't have enough wood because my sawmills are feeding the carpentry but nothing is feeding the sawmills."_ The long-term pull is completeness. You feel the shape of the graph fill in — each new tech reveals a new column of buildings, each new building reveals new edges, and the headline moment is not a big number but a wide one: a dashboard with eight resources, all positive per-second rates, all feeding something, no red arrows. There is no compression here; there is weaving.

### Explorer

Explorer is the archetype where _the map is the content_. You start in one sector with a fog of unknown around you, spend a stamina-like action resource to scout the next tile, and watch the world literally enlarge as you move. The first fifteen minutes are onboarding-via-cartography: you are learning what the symbols mean by uncovering them, and every step is a small reveal. Scarce resources (food, water, scrap metal) force you back to a base tile, then out again, and the session texture becomes a heartbeat of expedition → retrieve → refit → expedition.

A thirty-minute session is a widening circle punctuated by equipment moments. You find an item — a knife with +2 scavenge, a canteen that doubles water capacity, a torch that turns a dark sector survivable — and that item reshapes where on the map you can reach. The long-term pull is completion of a _place_: every sector eventually visited, every landmark investigated, every passage between levels charted. Unlike Looper, your progress does not reset at the end of a run; unlike Scaler, it does not compress. The map you charted an hour ago is still charted. The reward is territory, not a number.

### Scripter

Scripter is the archetype where _the player is the author of the game's behaviour_. You do not click buttons to advance — you write a program that clicks for you, and progress is the diff between what your script can describe and what the game currently demands of you. The first fifteen minutes are onboarding-via-vocabulary: you learn a tiny command set (`buy`, `wait`, `if`), type a four-line script, hit run, and the screen flashes as a state mutation happens without your hand on the mouse. From that moment, every decision is a rewrite.

A thirty-minute session is a sequence of edits, runs, and observes. You notice a resource that stalls, edit the script to add a conditional, run it, watch the execution log, tighten the condition, run it again. The long-term pull is _the program plays the game better than you can_ — not a number, not a territory, a proof. End-of-arc is when your script executes a multi-step strategy you could not personally hand-dispatch inside the arc's time budget. Unlike Looper, the queue is not drag-edited — it is authored in text (or in a spatial command vocab with control-flow primitives) and re-authored when the world demands something the old script cannot name. Unlike Producer, the scaling axis is script quality, not purchase count. The Bitburner player's boast — _"my first script was fifty lines, my hundredth is ten"_ — is the archetype-signature mastery metric.

### Phase-shifter

Phase-shifter is the archetype where _the game changes itself_. You do not prestige; the mechanics you just mastered are retired, replaced, or absorbed into a new mode the player did not know existed. The first ten minutes are deliberately sparse — a single button, a minimalist log, a narrative copy hook that raises a question and does not answer it. You learn one loop and then, without warning, a new affordance appears in the margin. The mystery is doing work the tutorial normally does.

A thirty-minute session is a sequence of discoveries. Each phase has its own rhythm — Phase 1's anxious fire-stoking, Phase 2's wire-price optimization, Phase 3's dusty-path inventory triage. The signature moment is the _pivot_: the second when the game reveals it was not what you thought. "Wait, this is a village sim now?" "Wait, this is an RPG?" "Wait, this is an existential argument about paperclips?" Unlike Producer, there is no central number you chase for the whole arc; unlike Looper, the loops are not cyclical — each phase is a one-way door. Unlike Scaler, the layer you add does not accelerate the old one; it replaces it, or sidelines it, or absorbs it into a larger machine. The long-term pull is _narrative momentum_ — not "I want to see a bigger number" but "I want to see what happens next." The arc-closure beat is the moment the game stops pretending it is a single genre and the player realises, often in retrospect, that the whole structure was a phased argument.

### Ambient

Ambient is the archetype the player does not play so much as _keep_. You open it once in the morning, glance at it while a pot boils, check it on the way back from a meeting, let it run at the edge of a monitor for an afternoon. Resources accumulate whether you are watching or not. Nothing expires. No wave is about to overrun your defenses. No cooldown queue is about to waste. The game has committed mechanically to never punishing absence — `scaled-opposition` and every cooldown urgency are deliberately refused, not merely absent. What remains is production that runs on its own, `automation` buying the next thing, a slowly unfolding unlock tree, and optional softened `prestige`. The player's relationship to the game is _companionship_, not engagement: the game lives on the desktop the way a plant lives on a desk.

A thirty-minute session is actually seven three-minute glances distributed across the day. Each glance yields accumulated output, one or two affordable purchases, and occasionally a new visual element revealed (a building appears, an animal wanders in, a zone quietly unlocks). The long-term pull is not mastery or compound growth — it is the pleasant, legible, ongoing state of the game: _my thing is running, it is happy, it grew while I was away._ The commitment is old — Anti-Idle made it in the web era — and the desktop-companion cluster around Rusty's Retirement remade it; what changes between the two is the visual budget, not the engagement contract.

### Tug

Tug is the archetype where you and the opponent feed the same lane. You spend income on unit production; the units auto-walk toward the opponent's base; theirs auto-walk toward yours; when the two streams meet somewhere in the middle they resolve combat without commands, and the survivors keep walking. The front-line position drifts toward whichever side is losing DPS races, and the match ends when one base's HP reaches zero. The player's decisions are composition (what unit types to buy), timing (send now or bank for a larger wave), counter-reading (opponent just massed light armor — pivot to splash damage), and upgrade allocation (income upgrades now delay presence now but pay off later). Moment-to-moment APM is low by design; strategic depth lives in the composition decision space and in reading the opponent's output, since every unit they send is visible evidence of their intentions.

A first 1–2 hours of play unfolds across 2–4 matches (each 15–30 minutes). First match is onboarding — learning the shop, the send affordance, what a winning front-line push looks like. Second match is where counter-picking shows up: the player starts losing a composition matchup and learns to pivot. Third match is where timing discipline emerges: income upgrades must be scheduled against opponent pressure, not in abstract. The long-term pull is the unbounded depth of the counter-type matrix and opponent variety — a fixed unit roster feels shallow after three matches against AI, but against a second live player the space becomes combinatorial because the opponent adapts. The archetype's signature pleasure is the moment after a bad early-game composition when a well-timed pivot reverses the front line in one send; the corresponding pain is the moment when your opponent recognizes your tank-heavy push and drops splash damage before your tanks arrive. Both are compositions of the same structural tension — you are authoring pressure against another agent authoring pressure in the same shared space.

### Director

Director is the archetype where _you don't play the game — you tune it and watch it play itself_. Four factions spread across a symmetric map under fully autonomous AI; the player is one of them in name only. What the player actually does is occupy a handful of goal slots with strategic intents (_"claim the north hotspot," "defend the east sector," "pressure the blue faction"_) and spend meta-currency between rounds on permanent upgrades. Everything else happens on its own. Tendrils creep, frontiers clash, hotspots flip, the round clock runs down. When it ends, the map resets and the upgrade tree advances.

A thirty-minute session is four to five rounds — each round a ~five-minute sprint of _read the board, adjust goals, watch it play out, tally the meta-currency, spend, reset_. The pull isn't "I am winning a fight" — it's _"I am learning a system"_. Round one feels chaotic; round eight the player can predict where their frontier will stall and reshape their goal stack to compensate; round twenty, they're tuning a deterministic-enough machine that their upgrade priority decides the outcome. The arc closure beat — a full 5-slot toolkit — arrives around the two-hour mark, and the game's content at that point is the diff between the player's first and last rounds: the same simulation, twenty rounds of accumulated direction.

### Survivor

Survivor is the archetype of _standing inside the threat_. You control a single character on a scrolling arena; enemies pour in from the edges and close toward you; your weapons fire automatically on cooldown and your only direct inputs are movement and per-level-up item choices. The texture of play is continuous reactive positioning — kite the horde, catch the XP gems, turn to feed the oncoming wave into your weapon's firing arc. Every thirty to ninety seconds, a level-up pauses combat and offers three or four item cards; you pick one, then the arena resumes with the threat a little denser and your build a little sharper. The player role is _participant_, not architect.

A thirty-minute session is roughly one complete run. Enemy composition escalates on a minute-mark schedule; by mid-run you are chasing specific weapon-passive pairings that trigger named _evolutions_ — qualitative end-states that turn a level-8 base weapon into a distinct, stronger form. The run ends on a timer or on death, and run-earned currency flows into a between-runs shop of permanent bonuses. The long-term pull is the unlock cascade — new characters, weapons, stages, and modifier cards that reshape what future runs can build toward — plus a mechanical compound: each Power-Up makes the next run a little easier, so yesterday's 20-minute death becomes today's 30-minute completion.

### Cycler

Cycler is the archetype of _breathing in and breathing out_. The game divides into two named phases — a calm build phase where you place infrastructure on a shared map and spend what you've earned, and a pressure phase where whatever you built gets tested against an escalating threat. The phases share everything: the same coin pile, the same walls, the same fortress topology. Phase A sets the board; Phase B reveals whether the board holds. The rhythm is cyclical — day becomes night becomes day, round becomes wave becomes round — and each cycle raises the stakes, so Phase A must outpace Phase B's escalation just enough to stay ahead.

A thirty-minute session spans three to six full cycles. The signature moments are the transitions — the sunset, the "wave incoming" countdown, the horn before the assault — and the inspection beats that follow a survived wave ("the east wall held; reinforce the north"). Unlike Producer you are not chasing a central number; unlike Defender you are not committing once to an architecture and watching it auto-resolve; unlike Survivor you are not inside the threat. You are the architect during Phase A and the auditor during Phase B, and the long-term pull is the compounding payoff of shared-state investment: the fortress that survived ten nights is _yours_ in a way that no re-rolled run can match. Sessions end when the cycle finally overruns you, or — in campaign-closure variants — when the final wave is cleared and the run-arc closes.

### Packer

Packer is the archetype of _arranging a bounded space so that position becomes power_. You acquire items — gems, inventory pieces, crafting components — and you place them inside a fixed grid. Each placement is a small decision about what sits next to what; the synergy is never in the item alone but in the shape its neighbours make. You hover an item over an open cell and the board lights up showing "+30% from three matching neighbours"; you try another cell and the bonus halves. You rotate a tetromino shape to fit it against the chain you already built, or you stop, sell one item back, and rearrange three others because you just noticed the better layout. The texture of play is _spatial hypothesis_ — a quiet puzzle surface that rewards looking before committing, and where the combat or production tick that follows is the score.

A thirty-minute session is one full run — arrange, resolve, adjust, repeat for six to twelve rounds depending on sub-variant. The signature moments are the compositional discoveries: the first time a neighbour bonus multiplies unexpectedly, the first combine-up that merges two lesser items into a qualitatively new form, the first wave (in Defender-hybrid variants) that wipes because the arrangement failed and you see exactly which cell was wrong. The long-term pull is the solution space: every new item acquired opens new arrangements, and the item pool is large enough that exhaustive search is impractical — players genuinely encounter combinations that nobody documented. The player role is _arranger_, not architect (that's Defender) and not participant (that's Survivor).

### Skirmish

Skirmish is real-time strategy. Each faction runs an economy, spends it building an army, and sends that army across a shared continuous field to break its rivals' bases — all of it at once, in real time, with no turn boundary to hide behind. The classic RTS tension is the whole game: income spent on the economy compounds and income spent on the army does not, so a faction that over-invests is defenceless when pressure arrives and one that under-invests wins the first engagement and loses the war. Build order, army composition and when to commit are the decisions, and they are decisions _because_ rivals are making them at the same time against you.

The field holds **N factions, not a duel** — that is the line between this and Tug, which is bilateral zero-sum over one shared lane by construction. Here three armies can meet, the weakest two can be left alone to bleed each other while the third expands, and a faction that wins an engagement can still emerge too thin to survive the next arrival. Threat is directional and unevenly distributed, so _who to fight_ sits alongside how much to build. Free-for-all is the base case; teams and alliances are the same substrate with a friend/foe relation over it.

Combat resolves on an open field rather than a lane or a grid. Units acquire targets, close, and fight; a front forms wherever armies happen to meet. Position is consequential without being authored — a stream of arrivals is defeated piecemeal by a mass that arrived together, so _when_ an army is committed matters as much as how large it is.

A session is a match, and a match is an argument between build orders. The early minutes are quiet and economic; first contact tells you whether your split was right; the recovery — or the failure to recover — is the rest of the match. The long-term pull is the strategy space: economy-versus-army is one axis, composition another, target selection a third, and against rivals adapting on the same axes there is no single correct answer to converge on. Skirmish ships with AI on every seat, which makes it fully playable and fully balanceable before a human seat exists; a player seat is a policy swapped for input.

### Auto-battler

Auto-battler is the archetype of _front-loaded commitment followed by watched resolution_. Each round opens with a thirty-second prep phase: the shop refreshes five unit offers drawn from a pool you share with seven opponents, and you spend the gold you've accumulated. You buy units — competing against opponents for the same limited supply — position them on your half of the board, and compose a roster whose class and trait tags cross synergy thresholds. You fish for copies to combine-up into higher-tier stars. You decide whether to spend now or hoard gold for the interest bonus at round-end. Then the timer runs out, combat resolves automatically in real time, and you watch. Your units move, attack, and trigger abilities according to whatever rules you composed them for; you cannot intervene. The outcome is the score of a decision you finished making before combat started.

A thirty- to forty-minute session is one full match — eight to twelve rounds, escalating from PvE creep waves through PvP brackets until seven of eight players are eliminated. A full first-completion arc spans one match plus the beginning of a second, so the player sees the compositional surface resolve against opponents _and_ feels the meta-progression pull on the follow-up run. The signature moments are the draft-board tensions (the key piece you need was bought by opponent three; do you pivot or reroll?), the synergy-threshold triggers (the third Knight drops and the armour bonus cascades across your line), and the round where your preparation visibly delivered. The long-term pull is the synergy-chart mastery — memorising which tag thresholds stack, which piece orderings counter which compositions, which economic patterns (hoard-for-interest vs level-up-fast) dominate the current patch. The player role is _drafter_, not architect (that's Defender) and not arranger (that's Packer).
