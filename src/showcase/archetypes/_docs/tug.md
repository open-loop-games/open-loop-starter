## Player experience

Tug is the archetype where you and the opponent feed the same lane. You spend income on unit production; the units auto-walk toward the opponent's base; theirs auto-walk toward yours; when the two streams meet somewhere in the middle they resolve combat without commands, and the survivors keep walking. The front-line position drifts toward whichever side is losing DPS races, and the match ends when one base's HP reaches zero. The player's decisions are composition (what unit types to buy), timing (send now or bank for a larger wave), counter-reading (opponent just massed light armour — pivot to splash damage), and upgrade allocation (income upgrades delay presence now but pay off later). Moment-to-moment APM is low by design; strategic depth lives in the composition decision space and in reading the opponent's output, since every unit they send is visible evidence of their intentions.

A first hour of play unfolds across a dozen or so matches of a few minutes each. First match is onboarding — learning the shop, the send affordance, what a winning front-line push looks like. Second match is where counter-picking shows up: the player starts losing a composition matchup and learns to pivot. Third match is where timing discipline emerges: income upgrades must be scheduled against opponent pressure, not in abstract. The signature pleasure is the moment after a bad early-game composition when a well-timed pivot reverses the front line in one send; the corresponding pain is the moment your opponent recognizes your tank-heavy push and drops splash damage before your tanks arrive. Both come from the same structural tension — you are authoring pressure against an opponent authoring pressure in the same shared space.

Between matches, a career survives. Experience earned in each match raises a level that buys permanent upgrades and opens the roster, so a lost match still moves the player forward and the next one starts from a stronger table. That surviving layer is what makes Tug an incremental expression rather than a one-lane Skirmish: the in-match economy is the same contest, and the career over it is the part that compounds.

## Lineage

Legion TD 2 and the StarCraft II arcade custom Direct Strike are the lane-push market this line descends from. Both are out: nothing earned by play survives a match beyond ranked standing and cosmetics. Legion TD 2 is sourced; its `statistics` drive only ranked placement.

## Against Skirmish

Tug and Skirmish share an economy split between growth and units, auto-resolving combat, a counter wheel and persona-driven opponents. Lane versus open field, two seats versus N, and a human seat versus an AI opponent are each line's own choices, not the boundary between them. The boundary is the tier: Tug keeps a career across matches and is core; Skirmish keeps nothing and is adjacent.
