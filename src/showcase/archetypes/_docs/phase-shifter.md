## Player experience

Phase-shifter is the archetype where _the game changes itself_. You do not prestige; the mechanics you just mastered are retired, replaced, or absorbed into a new mode the player did not know existed. The first ten minutes are deliberately sparse — a single button, a minimalist log, a line of narrative copy that raises a question and does not answer it. You learn one mechanic and then, without warning, a new affordance appears in the margin. The mystery is doing work the tutorial normally does.

A thirty-minute play session is a sequence of discoveries. Each phase has its own rhythm — Phase 1's anxious fire-stoking, Phase 2's wire-price optimization, Phase 3's dusty-path inventory triage. The signature moment is the _pivot_: the second when the game reveals it was not what you thought. "Wait, this is a village sim now?" "Wait, this is an RPG?" "Wait, this is an existential argument about paperclips?" Unlike Producer, there is no central number you chase for the whole arc; unlike Looper, the phases are not cyclical — each phase is a one-way door. Unlike Scaler, the layer you add does not accelerate the old one; it replaces it, or sidelines it, or absorbs it into a larger machine. The long-term pull is _narrative momentum_ — not "I want to see a bigger number" but "I want to see what happens next." The arc-closure beat is the moment the game stops pretending it is a single genre and the player realises, often in retrospect, that the whole structure was a phased argument.
